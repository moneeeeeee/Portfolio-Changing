PneumoDetect: Pneumonia Detection from Chest X-rays

Individual project | Python, TensorFlow/Keras, VGG16, pydicom

Overview

A VGG16 classifier with DICOM input checks and an FDA-style submission. A study in honest evaluation: it is not clinically ready, and the write-up explains why.

Could a transfer-learning model flag pneumonia on frontal chest X-rays well enough to assist a radiologist? The NIH dataset has 112,120 images from 30,805 patients, but pneumonia appears in only 1.28% of them, so a model can score high accuracy while missing nearly every case.

Role

Individual project. Udacity AI in Healthcare Nanodegree (June – Aug 2026).

Description

  • Analysed prevalence, demographics, view position, comorbidities and pixel intensities before modelling.
  • Split the data 80/20 by patient, because patients averaged 3.64 images each and an image-level split would leak.
  • Found that pneumonia cases were more often AP views (56% against 40%), so I stratified by view and kept view position out of the model inputs.
  • Compared class weighting with balanced downsampling and chose downsampling for more stable training.
  • Trained a frozen VGG16 with a new classification head, and chose the threshold by sweeping F1 on a validation set kept at real-world prevalence.
  • Wrote three DICOM checks (body part, patient position, image type) that reject out-of-scope studies before inference.
  • Wrote an FDA-style submission stating intended use, limitations and risks.

Why this model is not ready for clinical use

PneumoDetect runs end to end, but I would not put it in front of a radiologist. At its best operating point it finds 25 of 277 pneumonia cases, about 1 in 11, and raises roughly 27 false alarms for every true detection. Accuracy looks high at about 96% only because almost 99% of the images have no pneumonia. A tool that misses most cases and is usually wrong when it does flag one would add reading work without adding safety.

  1. The labels are noisy. The NIH labels were mined from radiology reports by natural language processing, not confirmed by radiologists reading the images. Pneumonia is hard to call on a chest X-ray and readers disagree on it, so both training and evaluation carry label error.
  2. Pneumonia rarely appears alone. In my analysis, 42% of pneumonia images also showed infiltration, followed by edema (24%), effusion (19%) and atelectasis (18%). Pixel-intensity distributions for pneumonia, infiltration and normal images overlapped heavily, so the model can learn the companion finding instead of pneumonia.
  3. There were few positive examples. Only 1,431 of 112,120 images (1.28%) are pneumonia. Balancing the classes left 2,308 training images, which is small for a deep network, and the validation loss was unstable from epoch to epoch.
  4. The network was not adapted to X-rays. I used VGG16 as a frozen feature extractor with a small new classification head. Its features come from everyday photographs, and none of its layers were fine-tuned on radiographs.
  5. The validation is not clinical validation. Performance was measured on a held-out portion of the same dataset, with the same noisy labels. There was no external hospital, no radiologist-adjudicated reference standard, and no testing by scanner, site or patient subgroup.

What it would take

  • A reference standard built from several board-certified radiologists reading each validation image, with majority vote and, where available, clinical or laboratory confirmation.
  • More confirmed pneumonia cases, and fine-tuning of the network (or a backbone pretrained on chest radiographs) instead of frozen features.
  • External validation on images from other hospitals and scanners, reported by view position, age and sex.
  • A sensitivity target agreed with clinicians before choosing the threshold, since a missed pneumonia costs more than a false alarm.

Outcome

What the project does demonstrate is the process around the model: choosing metrics by clinical consequence, preventing leakage, catching a confounder, guarding the input, and stating limits plainly. It taught me to report what a model cannot do as carefully as what it can.
